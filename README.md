In recent years, air pollution has had an increasingly severe negative impact on people’s health and quality of life. To meet this challenge effectively, government bodies and environmental organizations urgently need precise, detailed air‐quality data to inform scientific decision‐making and policy development. By gathering and integrating historical air‐quality records from multiple cities, and then applying advanced data‐analysis techniques and modeling methods, researchers can uncover how air quality varies across time and space. These insights not only help us understand pollution sources and dispersion mechanisms but also provide strong data support and scientific justification for implementing control measures and forecasting future air‐quality changes—ultimately contributing to environmental protection and public health improvement.

This project uses web‐scraping techniques to collect historical air‐quality data for selected cities and then applies data‐analysis and machine‐learning methods to investigate patterns and build predictive models. We focus on the adverse health effects of air pollution and emphasize the critical role of accurate air‐quality data in guiding scientific decisions and policies. As a concrete example, we scraped data from the “Weather Post” website for Ningbo City covering January through October 2024. Using Python, we retrieved over 250 records and then preprocessed them by removing duplicates, handling missing values, detecting and correcting outliers, and converting data types as needed.

During the exploratory‐analysis phase, we examined the data’s overall distribution, temporal characteristics, and spatial features. We described the distributions of the Air Quality Index (AQI) and major pollutant concentrations, and highlighted differences in air‐quality grades among cities. Visualizations further illustrated the temporal and spatial patterns of air quality as well as the profiles of the main pollutants, offering powerful tools for analyzing pollution trends, spatiotemporal distribution, and pollutant contributions.

For the machine‐learning modeling portion, we turned to Beijing’s air‐quality dataset and selected PM2.5, PM10, SO₂, NO₂, CO, and O₃ as predictor variables. We trained three regression models—Linear Regression, Gradient Boosting Regression, and Random Forest Regression—to forecast the AQI. The experiments showed that the Gradient Boosting model delivered the best performance, achieving the lowest mean squared error and the highest coefficient of determination. Scatter plots and residual‐distribution histograms further confirmed the model’s predictive accuracy and stability.

In summary, through careful design and implementation, this project successfully achieved comprehensive collection, processing, and analysis of air‐quality data, providing robust tools for understanding and forecasting air quality. Its achievements not only offer valuable methods and insights for the field of air‐quality research but also supply a scientific basis for government agencies and environmental organizations when crafting policies and interventions. In the future, we plan to expand the dataset’s scope, explore more advanced machine‐learning algorithms, and apply our results in real‐world air‐quality management strategies to enhance model generalization and prediction accuracy, thus supporting the development of more effective air‐quality control policies.
